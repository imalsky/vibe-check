---
title: "Vibe-Check: Reliability checks for machine-learning surrogates in scientific software"
date: 2026-08-01
author: "Isaac Malsky"
---

Machine-learning surrogate models are now common across numerous and disparate scientific disciplines. These models show promise for speeding up complex, costly numerical simulations. Ideally, a machine-learning emulator reproduces what a classical physical solver computes, but at a fraction of the cost. 

Weather and climate models are a good example of how far this has come. GraphCast now beats traditional numerical weather prediction on most benchmarks (Lam et al. 2023), and NeuralGCM couples a learned physics core directly into a general circulation model (Kochkov et al. 2024). These methods break the usual accuracy-speed tradeoff, and that opens the door to a new wave of science: higher-resolution models, more physics per simulation, and broader parameter studies than anyone could previously afford.

That promise only matters if you can trust the output. A surrogate that quietly gets the wrong answer is worse than a slow solver that gets the right one, because a plausible-looking wrong answer is much harder to catch. Trust isn't a nice-to-have here. It's the whole point: the moment a group can't stand behind what a model predicts, the speedup stops mattering.

Right now, most surrogates get a fraction of the scrutiny they deserve. Often the only real test is the held-out test-set error, maybe with a few example plots as a sanity check. That is not enough, especially in high-dimensional problems. A model with a great average error can still fail badly in specific regions of parameter space. It can silently extrapolate past anything it was trained on. It can violate physical constraints, like conservation or positivity, that the original solver respected by construction. And it can fail quietly: the simulation keeps running, the numbers keep coming out, and nothing tells you they have gone wrong. Once a surrogate is buried inside a larger code, every downstream module inherits that failure, and by the time someone notices, it can be nearly impossible to trace back to its source.

Every emulator author also quietly makes a pile of design choices that shape the final model: what normalization to use for each channel, how to weight a loss across outputs that span wildly different units and scales, whether to enforce conservation, positivity, or other known constraints. Right now there's no consolidated way to see the consequences of any of these choices, which means a lot of models end up impossible to reproduce, compare, or even reuse outside the notebook they were trained in.

This isn't hypothetical. A survey of machine-learning-based science found leakage errors, one specific and common failure mode, in 17 different fields, affecting 294 published papers, in some cases leading to badly overoptimistic conclusions (Kapoor and Narayanan 2023). That's one failure mode, caught after publication, in work that presumably looked fine at the time. It's a safe bet that plenty more is still sitting undetected in results people are relying on right now.

I have written some version of these checks for nearly every emulator I have built, and thrown the code away each time. That is the itch behind this project. Prominent efforts like NeuralGCM, GraphCast, and ACE2 (Watt-Meyer et al. 2024) show how far surrogate modeling has come. Validation tooling has not kept up. General-purpose ML testing libraries exist. So do verification and uncertainty-quantification toolkits built for classical simulations. But none of them answer the questions that actually come up once a neural emulator is embedded in a physics code: Is the scaler honest? Is the model being asked to extrapolate? Does it still conserve what the solver conserved? Does the exported, deployed copy agree with the model that was actually trained?

So, over this URSSI fellowship, I am building Vibe-Check. Here's the pitch: an open-source Python package that runs a battery of reliability checks against a trained surrogate before anyone downstream has to take it on faith. Over the next six months I will:

1. Build the package to test normalization, data-split hygiene, training-domain coverage, constraint violations, calibration, exported inference, and speed.
2. Validate it against representative local, sequential, and spatial-field emulators.
3. Open-source the package, documentation, and tutorial material, so other groups can actually use it, not just read about it.

The goal is something you can hand a predict function and your data splits, numpy in and numpy out, and get back a report that someone who did not train the model can actually read, and trust. It's still early. Right now that report covers ten checks, and I expect the list to change once people outside my own head start using it and telling me what I got wrong.

Here's one concrete example of the kind of thing I want Vibe-Check to catch, because I think it's a good illustration of just how sneaky these failures can be: normalization leakage. Almost every surrogate normalizes its inputs, usually by subtracting a per-feature mean and dividing by a per-feature standard deviation. Those statistics are supposed to come from the training split alone. Fit them on the full dataset instead, train plus test, and you've let information about your held-out data leak into the pipeline before you ever evaluate it.

I built a small demonstration to show how bad this can get. One input feature is nearly constant in the training data, with a standard deviation of just 0.03, but it varies normally at test time. I trained the same model twice, changing exactly one thing: whether the scaler was fit on the training split alone, or on the full dataset. The honest pipeline reports a test RMSE of 0.87. The leaky one reports 0.25, 3.5 times better on paper, and every bit of that improvement is fake. Here's the mechanism: fit on training data alone, the scaler divides that feature's test values by 0.03, which blows them up to a scale of roughly thirty, far outside anything the model ever saw in training. Fit on everything, it divides by 0.59 instead, because the test set's own spread already pushed the standard deviation up, so the inputs stay in a reasonable range and the model looks great. It isn't. That flattering 0.25 cannot be reproduced by anyone re-running the workflow honestly, and it's hiding a real problem: this surrogate is not ready for inputs where that feature varies. Vibe-Check catches exactly this, by recomputing both sets of statistics and checking which one the scaler you actually used matches.
